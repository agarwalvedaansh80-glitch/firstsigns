1. My Craniosynostosis Journey and the Path to a Specialist

The first sign

The first sign was an eye. When I slept, my left eyelid would not close all the way. My mother noticed it soon after I was born in June 2010. She noticed two other things too: the left side of my head was flattened, and my nose sat a little crooked.

Our paediatrician said that babies come in all shapes and that most of these things settle on their own. For most babies, that is the right answer. My mother still felt that something was not right, and my parents kept asking.

The search for a diagnosis

When I was a month old, I was taken to more paediatricians. Because of my eye, they sent us to ophthalmologists. A sonography of the eye seemed to show a large mass behind it, and a biopsy was suggested. Frightened and unsure, my parents decided against it.

An MRI of my face and skull, and then a CT scan of my whole body, were done at Nanavati Hospital in Mumbai. The MRI report came after three days, said the case was difficult to understand, and suggested plagiocephaly, a common flattening of the head. The CT scan found nothing. Many relatives took that clear report as proof that everything was fine. But my head was still flattened, my nose was still crooked and my eye still did not close, so my parents kept carrying the reports and films from one radiologist to another while the months went by.

The word that changed everything came from an oncologist, who looked at everything and said it might be craniosynostosis. He referred us to a neurosurgeon. Neurosurgeons, neurologists, craniofacial surgeons and plastic surgeons followed. All of them said I needed surgery. None of them could yet tell my parents at what age, how many operations, or what would happen afterwards, so my parents also sent my reports to doctors outside India.

A stranger at dawn

Around my seventh month, my parents met Dr. Debraj Shome by chance, early one morning outside Mumbai airport. He listened to two worried parents and recommended a 3D colour MRI at Dr. Jankharia's imaging centre. That scan finally showed my condition clearly: the coronal suture on the left side of my skull had closed too early. That one early closure explained everything my mother had seen, the flat side of my head, the crooked nose and even the eye that would not close.

Choosing a surgeon

Through Facebook, my parents found an international craniosynostosis family support group based in the United States. For the first time, they could read the stories of families who had been through the same thing. After weeks of research, they shortlisted two surgeons, Dr. David Staffenberg in New York and Dr. Jeffrey Fearon in Dallas, and chose Dr. Fearon. He advised that the best age for my surgery would be between fifteen and eighteen months. Because the American winter was coming, it was planned for my fourteenth month.

Then came the paperwork: visas and travel plans for my parents' first trip to America. My paternal grandmother's visa was refused. My paternal aunt, my maternal grandfather and my late maternal grandmother travelled with my parents and me. In Dallas, for the first time, my family knew exactly what would happen before, during and after the operation.

Before the surgery

In the month before surgery, I was given three injections, one a week, to raise my haemoglobin, and my blood was tested before each one. Six needles in a month made me scared of anyone new, and doctors most of all. At the same time, I took my first steps and said my first words in Dallas, my favourite poem was "Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star", and I was dazzled by the red buses. My face and skull were measured and photographed so that the results could be compared afterwards.

The surgery

On the day of the surgery I was not allowed milk or water, and I was given anaesthesia so that the hospital team could take me from my parents easily. At Medical City Children's Hospital in Dallas, Dr. Jeffrey Fearon and Dr. David Sacco operated for more than three hours, reshaping the bones at the front and the back of my skull: an anterior cranial vault remodelling and a posterior split-skull cranioplasty. During the operation they found that the pressure inside my skull had already risen.

My own lost blood was recycled during the operation, so I needed no blood transfusion. A zig-zag incision was used so that the scar would hide in my hair, and absorbable stitches were used so that nothing had to be removed later.

Recovery and growing up

I was in hospital for only two days, because a hospital is not the safest place for a recovering child. My face swelled and turned dark for a week, and my eyes were almost shut, but I was active and wanted to play, which my family took as the best sign of all. After one week the swelling began to go down, and after one more week of precautions I flew home to India.

I went back to Dallas after one year, and then every four to five years, for photographs and measurements of my face and skull. Genetic testing showed that my case was sporadic, not inherited. I have never needed a second operation and have had no lasting complications. Today I am a student in Mumbai, and most people I meet have no idea any of this happened.

What I learned

When I look back, I do not see careless doctors. I see a gap between what a parent notices and what a busy system is trained to look for. My parents crossed that gap with persistence, luck, a support group found online and a chance meeting outside an airport. They had to draw their own map, one appointment at a time. I want the next family to be handed one.

The path from the first visible sign to specialist consultation

A general path for any family, shown next to what happened in my journey. It is not instructions for an individual child.

2. The Origin and Purpose of First Signs

Where First Signs comes from

First Signs did not begin as a plan. It began as a feeling I have carried for as long as I can remember: what it must have been like for my parents to know that something was wrong with their baby, and not to know where to go, who to ask or what came next.

I was born with craniosynostosis. For months, my family moved from doctor to doctor and from scan to scan, holding reports that disagreed with each other, while the time that mattered most for treatment slipped away. They found their way in the end through persistence, an online support group and a chance meeting with a doctor who stopped to listen. I was lucky. I grew up healthy, and most people I meet have no idea any of this happened.

But luck should not be the reason a child gets the right care. As I grew older, I looked at what an Indian family would find today if they searched the way my parents once did, and I found that very little had changed. Families abroad are given parent groups, clear guides and lists of specialists. Families in India are still mostly left to find their own way.

Why I started it

I started First Signs because I want to give back. I have been given a healthy life by the doctors who treated me and by parents who refused to stop asking. I want to use that to help other families, so that no parent in India has to face what mine did without information, without guidance and without knowing where to turn.

I want to bring a real change for families and for society: a change in awareness, so that the first signs are noticed early, and a change in navigation, so that every family knows the path from the first sign to the right specialist. If one family is spared the months of confusion my parents went through, First Signs will have done what it was meant to do.

Why it matters beyond one condition

Craniosynostosis is only one of many rare conditions that can be present before or at birth. They share the same failure: they are rarely seen, rarely taught and rarely screened for. The children affected are the ones whose treatment window narrows with every month of delay; the families carry the uncertainty, the cost and the searching; and doctors and hospitals cannot act on what they were never trained to expect. Craniosynostosis alone affects thousands of children born in India every year, and the same gap exists for families in many other countries.

That is why First Signs begins with craniosynostosis, the condition I lived, but is not limited to it. It is the starting point, not the boundary.

Our vision and mission

Our vision: a world, beginning with India, where no family faces craniosynostosis or any other rare pre-birth or birth defect alone, unaware or without direction.

Our mission: to build a medically governed awareness, education and family-navigation platform for craniosynostosis and other rare pre-birth and birth defects, beginning in India and reaching families globally, so that every family can recognise the first signs early and find the right specialist without delay.

What First Signs does

First Signs exists to help families, in India and beyond:

  • recognise when a baby's head shape, or another early sign, deserves a medical conversation, as early as possible;
  • understand craniosynostosis and other rare conditions in plain, reliable language;
  • know which kind of specialist to ask for, in what order, and what to take to the appointment;
  • prepare for each stage of treatment by knowing the right questions to ask; and
  • feel less alone, through clearly labelled family experience.

How First Signs works

First Signs is a medically governed awareness, education and family-navigation project of the Debabrata Mitalee Auro Foundation, and the Foundation is its institutional home.

  • Medically governed. Every medical statement is approved by the relevant subject specialist and by the Foundation's designated medical representative before it is published.
  • No diagnosis online. First Signs does not diagnose, prescribe or treat, and does not replace a consultation with a qualified doctor.
  • No rankings. Doctors and hospitals are never ranked, and no doctor is named without written consent.
  • Lived experience is labelled. Family stories are kept separate from medical information.
  • One condition first, then more. First Signs is proven on craniosynostosis first, and then, with the Foundation's written approval, widens to other rare pre-birth and birth defects.

3. Craniosynostosis Explained in Plain Language

The skull is not one bone

A baby's skull is made of several bony plates joined by flexible seams called sutures. The sutures stay open in the first years of life so that the skull can grow as the brain grows. The soft spots on a baby's head, called fontanelles, are where these seams meet.

What happens in craniosynostosis

In craniosynostosis, one or more sutures close earlier than they should. The skull cannot grow normally across that closed seam, so it grows more in other directions, and the head takes on an unusual shape. Sometimes the face, eyes or nose on one side are affected too. The shape depends on which suture has closed:

Suture that closes earlyHead shape a doctor may notice
Sagittal (top of the head, front to back)A long, narrow head. This is the most common type.
Coronal, one side (ear to top of the head)Flattening of one side of the forehead, sometimes with the eye and nose affected on that side.
Both coronal suturesA head that is broad and short from front to back.
Metopic (middle of the forehead)A narrow, triangular or pointed forehead.
Lambdoid (back of the head)Flattening on one side of the back of the head.

How common it is

Craniosynostosis is uncommon. The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention estimates that about 1 in every 2,500 babies is born with it. Most cases happen by chance. Some are part of a wider genetic condition, which the specialist team can assess.

Signs a parent may notice

  • a head shape that looks unusual and does not improve as the baby grows;
  • a forehead or back of the head that is flat or bulging on one side;
  • a ridge along part of the skull, or a soft spot that seems to close early;
  • on one side, an eye, eyebrow or nose that looks different from the other side.

Noticing one of these is a reason to ask a doctor, not a diagnosis.

Not every unusual head shape is craniosynostosis

Flattening caused by the way a baby lies, called positional plagiocephaly, is common and is a different condition that is usually managed without surgery. Telling the two apart needs an examination by a doctor, sometimes with a scan. It cannot be done from a photograph.

How it is diagnosed

A doctor examines the baby's head and asks how the shape has changed over time. If craniosynostosis is suspected, the baby is referred to a specialist, such as a paediatric neurosurgeon or a craniofacial surgeon, who may advise a scan to see which suture has closed.

How it is treated

Many children are treated with surgery, usually in the first year or two of life, to give the brain room to grow and to improve the head shape. Some very mild cases are only monitored. The right approach, and the right timing, are decided by the child's own specialist team, so it is worth asking them to explain the options in simple words.

After treatment

Children are followed up as they grow, so that the team can check the growth of the head and face. With timely treatment, most children go on to live full, ordinary lives.
