1. A Concise First-Person Account of My Craniosynostosis Journey

The first sign was an eye. When I slept, my left eyelid would not close all the way. My mother noticed it soon after I was born in 2010. She noticed two other things too: the left side of my head was flattened, and my nose sat a little crooked.

Our paediatrician said that babies come in all shapes and that most of these things settle on their own. For most babies, that is the right answer. My mother still felt that something was not right, so my parents kept asking. Over the next months I was seen by paediatricians, eye specialists and radiologists. One scan seemed to show a mass behind my eye. An MRI report suggested plagiocephaly, a common flattening of the head. A CT scan of my whole body found nothing.

The word that changed everything came from an oncologist, who said it might be craniosynostosis and referred us to a neurosurgeon. In my case, one of the joints in my skull, the left coronal suture, had closed too early. That one early closure explained everything my mother had seen. The specialists we met agreed that I needed surgery, but my parents still needed someone to explain at what age, how many operations and what would happen after.

Around my seventh month, my parents met Dr. Debraj Shome by chance, early one morning outside Mumbai airport. He listened, recommended a 3D MRI that finally showed my condition clearly, and guided my parents onward. With the help of an international family support group, my parents chose Dr. Jeffrey Fearon in Dallas, United States, and I had surgery there at fourteen months. For the first time, my parents knew exactly what would happen before, during and after the operation.

The surgery went well. I went back for follow-ups as I grew, genetic testing showed that my case was sporadic and not inherited, and I have had no lasting complications. Today I am a student in Mumbai, and most people I meet have no idea any of this happened.

When I look back, I do not see careless doctors. I see a gap between what a parent notices and what a busy system is trained to look for. My parents had to draw their own map, one appointment at a time. I want the next family to be handed one.

This is lived experience. It is not medical advice and is not a template for any other child.

2. The Origin and Purpose of First Signs

Origin. First Signs began with one family's experience of a late diagnosis. Years after my own surgery, I searched the internet the way my parents once had, as an Indian family would today. Families in the United States and the United Kingdom are given parent groups, surgery guides, specialist directories and helplines. In India, our research found very little of this. I founded First Signs to help close that gap, and the Debabrata Mitalee Auro Foundation agreed to take it forward as a Project of the Foundation at its Board meeting on 21 September 2026.

Purpose. First Signs helps families in India to:

  • recognise when a baby's head shape deserves a medical conversation;
  • understand craniosynostosis in plain, reliable language;
  • know which kind of specialist to ask for, and what to take to the appointment; and
  • feel less alone, through clearly labelled family experience.

What First Signs is not. First Signs does not diagnose, prescribe or treat, does not rank doctors or hospitals, and does not replace a consultation with a qualified doctor. Every medical statement is approved by the relevant specialist and by the Foundation's designated medical representative before it is published. First Signs begins with craniosynostosis and may, with the Foundation's written approval, extend to other rare conditions present at birth.

3. A Plain-Language Explanation of Craniosynostosis

The skull is not one bone. A baby's skull is made of several bony plates joined by flexible seams called sutures. The sutures stay open in the first years of life so that the skull can grow as the brain grows.

What happens in craniosynostosis. In craniosynostosis, one or more of these sutures closes earlier than it should. The skull cannot grow normally across that seam, so it grows more in other directions, and the head can take on an unusual shape. The shape depends on which suture has closed:

Suture that closes earlyHead shape a doctor may notice
Sagittal (top of the head, front to back)A long, narrow head. This is the most common type.
Coronal (one side, ear to top of the head)Flattening of one side of the forehead, sometimes with the eye and nose affected on that side.
Both coronal suturesA head that is broad and short from front to back.
Metopic (middle of the forehead)A narrow, triangular or pointed forehead.
Lambdoid (back of the head)Flattening on one side of the back of the head.

How common it is. Craniosynostosis is uncommon. The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention estimates that about 1 in every 2,500 babies is born with it.

Not every unusual head shape is craniosynostosis. Flattening caused by the way a baby lies, called positional plagiocephaly, is common and is a different condition. Telling them apart needs an examination by a doctor; it cannot be done from a photograph.

Treatment. Many children are treated with surgery, usually in the first year or two of life, to give the brain room to grow and to improve the head shape. Some very mild cases are only monitored. The right approach, and the right timing, are decided by the child's own specialist team.

4. The Patient and Family Journey: From the First Visible Sign to Specialist Consultation

StageWhat a family can do
1. NoticeA parent, grandparent or doctor notices something about the baby's head shape, forehead, soft spot or face that does not seem to settle with time.
2. Note it downWrite down when it was first noticed and whether it seems to be changing. Clear photographs taken over time, kept privately, can help the doctor see the change.
3. Ask the paediatricianMention the concern clearly at the next visit, or book a visit for it. It is always reasonable to ask: "Should a specialist take a look?"
4. ReferralIf the doctor thinks it is needed, the baby is referred to a specialist such as a paediatric neurosurgeon or a craniofacial surgeon.
5. Specialist consultationThe specialist examines the baby and may advise a scan. Take all earlier reports and the actual scan images, not only the written reports.
6. Understand the planAsk which suture is involved, whether treatment is needed, at what age, how many operations may be needed, and what happens before and after.
7. Keep a family fileKeep reports, scan images, referral letters and a written list of questions together, and take them to every appointment.
8. Find supportRecognised support organisations can help families feel less alone. Another family's story can give comfort, but it is not medical advice.

5. Verified Information and Navigation Gaps Identified Through Our Research

Before writing any page, we reviewed 13 international support organisations, 13 major hospital and public health websites, and the Indian landscape of hospitals, professional societies, family communities, government schemes, insurance and regional-language content. Where we say something was not found, the searches we ran are recorded. The main gaps for families in India:

Gap foundWhat it means for families
No dedicated Indian craniosynostosis parent community was found.Indian parents were posting in groups run from other countries.
No Indian hospital page was found that combines condition education with costs, schemes and family support.Families must piece together information from many places.
No family guide was found to any government funding pathway. Craniosynostosis is not among the nine birth defects named under RBSK, and no named PM-JAY package could be verified.Families do not know what to ask at a scheme or hospital desk.
No clear guidance was found on whether insurance covers craniosynostosis.Families cannot plan for costs early.
No craniosynostosis patient content was found from any Indian professional society.There is no trusted Indian reference written for parents.
Very little regional-language content was found, and none of it practical.Families who do not read English easily have almost nothing to rely on.
No Indian parent-experience stories or videos were found.New parents have no Indian examples to learn from.
No neutral directory of Indian craniofacial specialists was found.Families do not know which kind of specialist to look for.

Late presentation. A 2025 study from a tertiary centre in India found that children with craniosynostosis were first seen at an average age of about 10.9 months (Singh et al., 2025). This is often later than the age at which the least invasive treatment options are generally considered, which is why earlier recognition matters.
