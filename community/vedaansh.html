Vedaansh's journey

"I was once the child whose parents were looking for answers."

Vedaansh Agarwal was born in 2010 with left unilateral coronal craniosynostosis. This is his family's own account of the fourteen months from his birth to his surgery, and of what came after. It is shared with the family's consent as lived experience.

Our journey, step by step

  1. At birth

    His parents noticed three things straight away: the left side of his head looked flattened, his nose was slightly crooked, and his left eyelid did not close fully when he slept. The paediatrician said everything was normal. His mother was not convinced.

  2. The first month

    The family went from one paediatrician to the next, and then to eye specialists. A sonography showed what looked like a mass behind the eye, and a biopsy was suggested. His parents decided against the biopsy and asked for further imaging first.

  3. Scans, and a name for it

    An MRI, reported after three days, described plagiocephaly. A full body CT scan found nothing of concern. After several radiologists, it was an oncologist who first suggested craniosynostosis and referred the family to a neurosurgeon.

  4. Many opinions, few explanations

    Neurosurgeons, neurologists, and craniofacial and plastic surgeons all advised surgery, but few could explain what the surgery would involve. The family sent the reports abroad for further opinions. Dr. Debraj Shome met them early one morning outside Mumbai airport to look at the reports, and on his recommendation a 3D MRI was done when Vedaansh was about seven months old.

  5. Finding a surgeon

    Through an international craniosynostosis family support group on Facebook, based in the USA, the family learned about specialist centres and shortlisted two craniofacial surgeons, one in New York and one in Dallas. Not every family member's visa came through, so an aunt and his maternal grandfather travelled with them. They chose Dr. Jeffrey Fearon in Dallas.

  6. The weeks before surgery

    Dr. Fearon advised surgery between 15 and 18 months of age. It was done at 14 months, before winter set in. In the month before, Vedaansh had three injections to raise his haemoglobin, with blood tests alongside; for his parents, this was the most frightening part. In the USA he started talking and walking, loved reciting Twinkle Twinkle Little Star, and was amazed by the red buses. His face and skull were measured and photographed before the operation.

  7. Surgery day

    No milk or water that morning. He was given anaesthesia before being handed over to the surgical team. His own blood was recycled during the operation, so he did not need a transfusion. The zig zag incision was hidden in his hair and closed with absorbable stitches. The surgery took a little over three hours.

  8. Recovery

    He spent two days in hospital. For about a week his face was swollen and dark with bruising and his eyes were almost closed, yet he stayed active throughout. Two weeks later, the family flew home to India.

  9. Follow up

    Follow up visits came at one year, and then every four to five years, with photographs and measurements each time. Genetic testing described his case as sporadic rather than inherited.

  10. Today

    Vedaansh is a Grade 11 student in Mumbai and the Founder and Youth Ambassador of First Signs, which he is building with Dr. Debraj Shome and the Debabrata Mitalee Auro Foundation, so that the next Indian family who notices something about their baby's head does not have to search the way his family did.

Vedaansh's story is the reason First Signs begins with craniosynostosis.

But his story is not a template for another child. Every child is different. Every diagnosis is different. Every family receives advice based on its own medical circumstances.

Family stories on First Signs are labelled as lived experience. They can tell you what another family felt, what questions they asked and what helped them emotionally. They cannot tell you what your child's treatment should be.
