Causes and genetics

The first question almost every parent asks is not medical. It is personal: did I do something to cause this? The answer, from every major centre, is no.

You did not cause this

Craniosynostosis is a condition a child is born with. It is not caused by how a mother slept, what she ate, normal medicines taken as prescribed, how a baby was held, delivered or laid down. The Craniofacial Center in Dallas, whose director reviews First Signs content, says it plainly: mothers who have delivered babies with craniosynostosis should not feel guilty they did anything wrong during their pregnancy to cause this.

What do the major centres say causes it?

  • Usually, no single cause is found. Mayo Clinic: often the cause is not known; non-syndromic craniosynostosis, the most common type, is thought to involve a mix of genetic and environmental factors. The CDC and Seattle Children's say the same: in most infants the cause is unknown and the child is otherwise healthy.
  • Pressure on the head before birth may play a role. The Dallas centre notes the most common reason seems to be pressure on the head in the womb, supported by the observation that craniosynostosis is 2 to 3 times more common in twin pregnancies, and suggests some genes may make sutures more likely to fuse when compressed rather than causing fusion directly.
  • A minority of cases are genetic. Some cases result from a change in a single gene, most often as part of a genetic syndrome (see syndromic craniosynostosis). The ISUOG patient leaflet estimates at least 25 to 30 per cent of cases have a genetic basis; Headlines UK puts syndromic cases at about 25 per cent.
  • Studied risk factors are modest and not your fault. The CDC and Cleveland Clinic mention research on maternal thyroid disease treatment and the fertility medicine clomiphene citrate as studied risk factors. These are associations in research, not verdicts on any family, and neither is something a parent chose knowing any risk.
If the guilt keeps circling, say it out loud at your next appointment and let your own specialist answer it. Parents tell us that hearing "you did not cause this" from their child's surgeon matters more than reading it anywhere.

Sporadic or inherited?

Most craniosynostosis is sporadic: it appears with no family history, affects one suture, and the child is otherwise well. Johns Hopkins states that for these single-suture cases the chance of it happening again in another child born to the same parents is low. Vedaansh's own case was described by genetic testing as sporadic, not inherited.

A smaller group is familial or syndromic, linked to a change in a named gene. The genes most often involved, in order of frequency per CAPPSkids, are FGFR2, FGFR3, TWIST1 and FGFR1. The five most common syndromes (Apert, Crouzon, Pfeiffer, Muenke and Saethre-Chotzen) are inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern: an affected parent has a 50 per cent chance of passing the gene change to each child, and all five can also arise as a new gene change in a family with no history at all.

Genetic counselling and testing: who actually needs it?

  • Mayo Clinic describes genetic testing as something done when the care team suspects craniosynostosis is part of a genetic syndrome, for example when more than one suture is involved or other features are present.
  • GOSH states that for most isolated sagittal cases, genetic testing would not be helpful.
  • Ask your team: does anything about my child's pattern suggest a syndrome? Would testing change treatment or follow-up? What would a result mean for future pregnancies?
  • In India, genetic counselling is available at major centres; Amrita Kochi, for example, offers antenatal counselling for fetal craniofacial conditions within its paediatric craniofacial division.

Can it be seen before birth?

Occasionally, and usually late. Johns Hopkins notes prenatal detection is rare because routine ultrasound is not refined enough for this diagnosis; Seattle Children's calls it rarely visible on routine scans; CHOP notes some children are diagnosed ahead of time by ultrasound. Where a scan does raise the question, the ISUOG leaflet suggests families ask how it might affect delivery planning, and notes MRI and genetic tests are sometimes offered when a syndrome is suspected.

Key point
One suture fusing early says nothing about intelligence by itself. For isolated sagittal synostosis, GOSH states children are usually of normal intelligence, and the syndromes each have their own picture, covered plainly on the syndromic page.
