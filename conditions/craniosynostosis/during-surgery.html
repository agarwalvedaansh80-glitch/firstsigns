During surgery: the day of surgery and the hospital stay

The operation day is usually harder for the parents than the recovery is for the child. Knowing the rhythm of the day helps.

The shape of the day

Admission and final checks

Paperwork, consent, weight and observations. Bring the folder.

Fasting window

Exact times set by the team; clear fluids often allowed until closer to surgery.

The anaesthetic room

A specialist paediatric anaesthetist; at many centres a parent can come in until the child is asleep.

The operation

Often a few hours; ask how and when you will get updates.

Recovery, then closer observation

Many children spend the first night in intensive or high-dependency care.

The ward and discharge

Swelling peaks around day two or three, then settles; written discharge instructions before you leave.

Most teams describe a similar arc: admission and final checks; a fasting period the team will specify; the anaesthesia conversation; the operation itself, often a few hours; then a recovery unit, and for many children a night of closer observation before moving to a regular ward. Ask your own team to walk you through their version of this day, including how and when you will receive updates while the operation is under way.

Questions for the anaesthesia and surgical team

  • How long do you expect the operation to take, and how will we get updates?
  • Where do we wait, and who will come to speak to us afterwards?
  • Will our child go to an intensive care or high dependency unit first?
  • What will our child look like immediately afterwards, so we are prepared?
  • When can we be with our child again?

Established topics families can ask their team about

These are established, published approaches used in craniofacial surgery in different centres. They are presented here as questions to discuss with the treating team, never as recommendations. Whether any of them applies to your child is entirely your team's judgement.

Blood conservation and cell salvage

Some teams use cell salvage, which recycles the child's own blood during surgery, to reduce the need for donor transfusion. You can ask: does your team use any blood conservation approaches, and how is blood arranged here?

Pre-operative erythropoietin

Some centres give erythropoietin injections before surgery to boost the child's own red blood cells and reduce transfusion needs. You can ask: is this part of your practice, and would it be relevant for my child?

Absorbable stitches

Stitches that the body absorbs on its own can reduce follow-up removal visits. You can ask: what kind of stitches will be used, and will they need removal?

Zig-zag incisions

Some surgeons use wavy incision patterns intended to camouflage the scar as hair grows. You can ask: what will the incision look like, and how do you plan it with the future hairline in mind?

The hospital stay

Ask the ward team what each day should look like, what is normal (many families describe facial swelling that peaks in the first days), which changes should prompt you to call a nurse, and what has to happen before discharge. Write down the discharge instructions, and ask for them in writing too.

Key point
One parent cannot do this alone around the clock. If two adults can share the bedside in shifts, both will cope better, and the child will always have a rested person beside them.
