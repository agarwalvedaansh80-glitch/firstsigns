Treatment options explained

Treatment depends on the child's diagnosis, age, symptoms, anatomy, number of sutures involved, associated conditions and the specialist team's assessment.

Treatment options explained

Many cases of craniosynostosis are treated surgically, but the exact procedure and timing are individual decisions. Some milder cases may be monitored. In selected circumstances, less-invasive approaches may be considered, sometimes with postoperative helmet therapy.

The approaches families hear about

Monitoring

For some children, the team may recommend watching growth and shape over time before deciding on any procedure. Monitoring is an active decision with scheduled reviews, not the absence of care.

Open cranial vault remodelling

Open surgery in which the team reshapes skull bones. Generally available across a wider age range. What it involves for a specific child, including the shape of the incision, is the team's design.

Endoscopic surgery with helmet therapy

A less invasive operation through small incisions, generally considered for young infants, usually followed by months of helmet therapy to guide growth. The age window matters, which is why early assessment counts.

Key point
Why timing matters in India: a published Indian tertiary-centre series (2025) found children presenting at an average of about 10.9 months, often past the window in which less invasive approaches are generally considered. First Signs exists so that families reach assessment earlier and hear every option that applies to their child. An older child still has options; the right person to lay them out is a specialist team.

Also in use for specific situations: distraction osteogenesis, where the bone is moved gradually over weeks with a device (CHOP, Johns Hopkins), used mostly for posterior expansion and syndromic cases. Published risk framing worth knowing: GOSH states on its procedure sheets that the overall risk of a major neurological event or death is much less than one per cent.

Disclosed disagreement: The Craniofacial Center, Dallas, whose director reviews First Signs content, does not use the endoscopic-plus-helmet route and publishes its reasons, operating instead at 11 to 15 months with its own outcome data. Most other major centres offer endoscopic surgery as standard for young infants. Both positions are published; your child's team is the right judge between them for your child.

First Signs will not tell an individual family which operation to choose. Instead, we will help families understand the conversation they need to have with the treating team.

Questions may include

  • What is the goal of treatment?
  • Why is treatment recommended at this age?
  • What options are appropriate for my child?
  • What are the benefits, limitations and risks of each option?
  • What is the expected hospital stay and recovery?
  • What follow-up will be required?
  • Could additional treatment ever be needed?
  • Which specialists will be involved before and after surgery?

Families may hear unfamiliar terms such as cranial vault remodelling, craniofacial surgery, endoscopic surgery or helmet therapy. Ask the treating team to explain the terms in the context of your child.

A treatment decision should be based on the child's clinical assessment, not on a website, social-media post or another family's surgical story.
