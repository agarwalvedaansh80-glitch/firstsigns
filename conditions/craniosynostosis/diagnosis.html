Diagnosis and imaging

Diagnosis starts with the child, not the scan.

Diagnosis and imaging
On this page

  • Which test, and why
  • Signs of raised pressure that should not wait

A healthcare professional may examine the baby's head shape, skull contours, head growth and other clinical features. The history matters too: when the shape was noticed, how it has changed, and whether there are other symptoms or relevant medical findings. Experienced craniofacial teams describe examination as the heart of diagnosis, with imaging used to answer specific questions.

Imaging may then be considered. A CT scan can show detailed information about the skull, but the decision about whether imaging is needed, and which investigation is appropriate, belongs to the treating team.

What examiners look for, suture by suture

Knowing the vocabulary helps you follow the conversation. These published examination patterns come from The Craniofacial Center, Dallas, with corroborating descriptions from the NHS and CAPPSkids. They are for understanding your specialist's words, never for diagnosing at home.

Sagittal

A ridge that can be seen or felt along the top of the head; a skull wider at the front and narrower toward the back; head circumference often larger than expected. Sagittal is the most common single-suture type, about half of all cases per Johns Hopkins.

Metopic

A ridge down the centre of the forehead with a narrower, pointed forehead; eyes may look closer together. One caution CAPPSkids highlights: the metopic suture normally fuses in infancy, so a metopic ridge on its own is not a diagnosis.

Coronal (one side)

The forehead on the fused side sits further back; the eyebrow on that side rides higher, making the eye look more open; the top of the nose can angle toward the fused side.

Lambdoid

Flattening on one side at the back with a low bump behind the ear on the same side and a lower skull height on the flattened side. It is the rarest single-suture form, about 1 per cent of cases per Johns Hopkins, and the one most often confused with positional flattening.

Which test, and why

  • Examination alone can be enough. Seattle Children's and Nationwide state some cases are diagnosed by office examination; the Dallas centre puts examination accuracy in experienced hands at about 98 per cent and reserves imaging for uncertain cases.
  • CT confirms and plans. Where used, centres describe low-dose or ultra-low-dose protocols with 3D views (Nationwide). Ask whether a low-dose protocol is used and keep the images, not just the report.
  • Other tools exist. Mayo and Johns Hopkins list MRI and cranial ultrasound among the options; Texas Children's highlights radiation-free 3D surface photography for follow-up. Which tool answers the question at hand is your team's call.

Signs of raised pressure that should not wait

A minority of children develop raised pressure inside the skull. Johns Hopkins lists the signs teams watch for: a full or bulging fontanelle, unusual sleepiness or reduced alertness, very noticeable scalp veins, increased irritability, a high-pitched cry, poor feeding, projectile vomiting, increasing head circumference and developmental delays. If these appear, contact your team promptly rather than waiting for the next routine appointment.

Families should ask

  1. What are you trying to confirm or exclude?
  2. What does the examination suggest?
  3. Is imaging needed now?
  4. If imaging is recommended, what will it tell us?
  5. Who will interpret the imaging?
  6. Does my child need referral to a craniofacial or neurosurgical specialist?
  7. Should we bring the actual images as well as the written report?

If previous scans have been performed, keep both the reports and the images when available. Different specialists may need to review the original material. For families travelling between cities, ask whether records can be shared ahead of the visit, and whether a remote or video review of existing reports is possible before you book travel.

Key point
A scan is one part of diagnosis. It should be interpreted in the context of the child and the clinical question.
