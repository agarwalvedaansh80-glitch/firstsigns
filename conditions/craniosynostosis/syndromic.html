Syndromic craniosynostosis

About one in four children with craniosynostosis has it as part of a wider genetic condition. These children need more than one operation planned across childhood, and a team that looks after the whole child: airway, eyes, ears, hands, growth and learning.

Syndromic craniosynostosis

CHOP notes there are close to 90 syndromes associated with heritable craniosynostosis. Five account for most cases. All five are autosomal dominant (an affected parent has a 50 per cent chance of passing it on) and all five can appear as a brand-new gene change in a family with no history. What follows is education, not a checklist for diagnosing your own child; syndromic care is exactly where an experienced multidisciplinary team matters most.

Apert syndrome (FGFR2)

Roughly 1 in 60,000 to 1 in 120,000 births (Dallas centre figures).

  • Both coronal sutures fuse before birth; the skull is short front to back and wide side to side (GOSH).
  • The hallmark is fused fingers and toes (syndactyly); hand releases can often be completed in two operations, per the Dallas centre.
  • The midface grows more slowly; the airway can be narrow, and about half of children have some form of cleft palate (Dallas centre figures).
  • GOSH states most children have some learning disability or developmental delay, varying from mild to severe; support at school makes a real difference.
  • Skull surgery is staged across childhood; midface surgery typically comes years later.

Crouzon syndrome (FGFR2)

Birth prevalence about 15.5 per million (CAPPSkids).

  • Coronal sutures fuse early; the eyes appear prominent because the eye sockets are shallow.
  • Eye protection and regular eye checks matter: the Dallas centre reports optic nerve problems in up to 28 per cent of children if pressure goes unmanaged.
  • Sleep studies are described as essential; sleep apnoea is common and treatable.
  • Hands and feet are typically normal, a key difference from Apert and Pfeiffer.
  • The Dallas centre states most children with Crouzon syndrome have normal development and intelligence.

Pfeiffer syndrome (FGFR2, rarely FGFR1)

About 1 per 100,000 (CAPPSkids).

  • Three subtypes: Type I is the most common and mildest, typically with normal intelligence; Types II and III are more severe and need intensive early care at an experienced centre.
  • The telltale sign is broad thumbs and big toes, which distinguishes Pfeiffer from Crouzon.
  • Airway management and eye protection are early priorities in severe forms.
  • Care is staged across childhood, with skull surgery first and midface surgery later.

Saethre-Chotzen syndrome (TWIST1)

About 1 in 25,000 to 1 in 50,000 (CAPPSkids).

  • One or both coronal sutures fuse; GOSH describes a high forehead with a low front hairline and often droopy eyelids (ptosis).
  • Mild webbing of the second and third fingers is common.
  • GOSH: intelligence is usually normal, children usually do well at school, college and university, and the vast majority grow up to lead a normal life.
  • When surgery is needed it usually happens in the first years of life, more often for appearance than for pressure.

Muenke syndrome (FGFR3, the P250R change)

About 1 per 30,000, around 8 per cent of all craniosynostosis (CAPPSkids), making its gene change the single most common one found.

  • One or both coronal sutures fuse; presentation varies widely, and some carriers show no signs at all (GOSH).
  • Hearing is the standing care need: sensorineural hearing loss affects the majority of children, so regular hearing tests belong in the plan.
  • GOSH notes around 10 per cent of children have epilepsy, and some have mild learning disabilities.
  • Outlook per GOSH: good, with the vast majority growing up to lead a normal life.

What every syndrome shares

  • CAPPSkids' rule, which First Signs adopts: all syndromic and complex patients should be treated by an experienced multidisciplinary craniofacial team and followed through to adulthood.
  • More than one operation over childhood is normal and planned, not a sign something went wrong.
  • The Dallas centre stresses coordinating specialists so tests and procedures share anaesthetics wherever possible.
  • Eyes, ears, airway, sleep, hands and learning each get their own follow-up thread; ask your team who owns each thread and how often it is reviewed.
Key point
For Indian families: syndromic care concentrates in a handful of metro centres. When you contact a centre, ask specifically how many syndromic craniosynostosis children the team follows, which specialties sit in its clinic, and how care is coordinated for families travelling from other cities.
