Your journey

Everyday care

Between hospital visits, life is nappies, naps, school runs and relatives with opinions. This page is about that life.

Everyday care and family wellbeing

Sleep

Follow your team's guidance on positioning after surgery, and ask them directly what is allowed: tummy time, side sleeping, car seats and carriers. Write their answer down, because relatives will ask, and it helps to quote the team rather than argue.

Helmet care, if prescribed

If your team prescribes helmet therapy, ask: how many hours a day, how to clean it, how to watch the skin underneath, and when fittings are reviewed. A helmet is a prescribed device with follow-up, not a product to buy online. If a helmet was not prescribed, your child does not need one.

Scar care

Ask your team when the scar can be touched, washed and oiled, and whether sun protection matters for your child. Most scars fade and hide well in hair over time; the first weeks always look more dramatic than the result.

Feeding and growth

Nothing about craniosynostosis changes the fundamentals: regular feeding, growth checks and vaccinations continue as your paediatrician advises. Keep the routine paediatric visits going alongside the specialist ones.

Emotions: yours, not only the child's

Parents describe guilt, fear and exhaustion, often more intense than anything the child seems to feel. A published analysis of 366 parent forum posts found that most were seeking community, and that the heaviest feelings clustered around diagnosis, not surgery. Craniosynostosis is a condition a child is born with; it is not caused by something a parent did in pregnancy or by how a baby slept. If the weight of it stays heavy, tell your doctor how you are feeling; support for parents is part of the child's care too.

Fathers carry this too, and often silently; the UK charity Headlines runs a dedicated dads' network for exactly that reason. When the First Signs community opens, its emotional support space will be for both parents, and grandparents as well; in many Indian homes they are the third parent, and bringing them to one appointment often converts the household's worry into support.

Siblings

Brothers and sisters notice everything. Simple, honest words work: their sibling's head bones joined a little early, the doctors fixed it, and it is nobody's fault. Give siblings a role: the bandage inspector, the toy carrier. Included children cope better than protected ones.

School and other children

Most children return to ordinary school life. For a visible scar or helmet, agree a one-line answer with your child that they can own: "my head bones joined early and the doctors fixed it." Tell the class teacher the facts once, so questions get calm answers. If teasing happens, treat it as a school behaviour issue and involve the teacher early.

You are allowed to be frightened. You are allowed to ask questions. You are allowed to ask for another explanation. You do not have to navigate the journey alone.
