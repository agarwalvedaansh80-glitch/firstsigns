Finding the right specialist

Craniosynostosis can involve more than one medical discipline. Depending on the child and diagnosis, families may encounter paediatricians, craniofacial surgeons, paediatric neurosurgeons, radiologists, ophthalmologists, geneticists and other specialists.

The right pathway is not necessarily identical for every child. In India, this surgery is concentrated in a small number of metro centres, so many families will travel. That is normal, and planning for it early makes everything easier.

First Signs will therefore avoid publishing a ranking of doctors or hospitals. Instead, we maintain a verified directory of participating specialists and centres, with their specialty, location, credentials supplied by the professional, and the scope of their involvement with First Signs clearly stated.

Who does what on a craniofacial team

The core surgical pair

At experienced centres the operation is typically performed jointly: a craniofacial or plastic surgeon (shape and reconstruction) and a paediatric neurosurgeon (safe work around the brain). Gillette and Texas Children's both describe this pairing as the norm; it is a fair thing to ask any Indian centre about.

Around them

A paediatric anaesthetist plans the anaesthetic and pain relief; an ophthalmologist checks the eyes where needed; a geneticist joins for suspected syndromes; and paediatric, nursing and, at some centres, a named coordinator keep the whole plan connected. Ask: who coordinates my child's care, and who do I call between appointments?

Second opinions, without awkwardness

Major centres treat second opinions as routine; Seattle Children's and Cincinnati Children's advertise pathways for them. In India the same is possible with a little structure: tell the first team plainly that you would like another opinion before deciding, ask for copies of the reports and the actual scan images, and ask the second team to review the same material rather than repeating scans. A confident team does not resent the question, and if your local doctor and a specialist disagree, a structured second opinion is exactly how families resolve it.

When contacting a specialist, ask

  • What is your specialty?
  • How often does your team assess craniosynostosis or related craniofacial conditions?
  • Which other specialties are involved?
  • Who will coordinate the child's care?
  • What information should we bring?
  • What happens after the assessment?

If you are travelling from another city

  • Ask whether the scan and the specialist visit can be combined into one day; some centres arrange this for outstation families if asked in advance
  • Ask whether existing reports can be reviewed remotely, by video or by sharing records, before you book travel
  • Carry every report and the actual scan images, not only the summary letters
  • Ask what a full assessment visit costs in writing, so the trip has no surprises
  • Ask who to contact with questions after you return home

For India, First Signs also maps existing referral and child-health pathways. Government programmes may provide screening or referral support in defined circumstances, but families should verify current eligibility and local implementation.

Key point
The purpose of a directory is navigation, not endorsement. A name appearing on First Signs should never be understood as a guarantee of treatment outcome or as a substitute for the family's own medical decision.
