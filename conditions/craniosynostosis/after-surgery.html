After surgery: recovery at home

Surgery is not the end of the journey. Follow-up is part of care.

The exact recovery period and follow-up schedule vary by procedure, age and the child's individual circumstances. Your surgical team should explain what to expect and what signs require urgent medical attention.

Week by week: what published centre guidance describes

Assembled from the published family guidance of GOSH, Gillette, Seattle Children's and Johns Hopkins. Your own team's instructions always override anything here.

WhenWhat centres describe
Right after surgeryA turban-style dressing; sometimes small drains behind the ears; marked face and eye swelling, with eyes often swollen shut, especially after front-of-head procedures (Gillette). Frightening to see, expected by every team.
Days 1 to 3Swelling worsens for two to three days and then starts to improve (GOSH); first night in intensive or high-dependency care; drains typically out by day two; stronger pain relief only in the first day or so, then paracetamol and ibuprofen suffice for most children (Gillette).
Days 3 to 7Discharge for open procedures at roughly day 3 to 5 depending on the centre; bruising fades over the first week; GOSH allows gentle hair washing with mild shampoo from about day three after spring surgery.
Weeks 2 to 6Endoscopic patients are scanned and start helmet wear about two weeks after surgery (Seattle); first follow-up commonly at three to six weeks (Gillette, GOSH).
Months 1 to 12Reviews at roughly one, six and twelve months for open procedures (Johns Hopkins), three-monthly in the first year after endoscopic surgery, and frequent helmet reviews where one is worn.

Going home: the red flags that should prompt a call

Ask your team for their written list before discharge, and where it differs from this general one, theirs wins. Published centre guidance converges on calling the hospital promptly for:

  • Fever above the threshold your team sets
  • Redness, swelling, warmth or discharge spreading around the wound, or the wound opening
  • A bulging or newly tense soft spot, or swelling that worsens again after it had begun settling
  • Repeated vomiting, refusal of feeds, or signs of dehydration (far fewer wet nappies)
  • Unusual drowsiness, difficulty waking, inconsolable irritability or a high-pitched cry
  • Clear fluid leaking from the wound or nose
  • A seizure, or any behaviour that frightens you: with a recently operated child, calling is always the right decision

Scar and wound care

  • Follow the team's written wound instructions exactly; ask before applying anything, including oils and traditional preparations.
  • Most modern teams use absorbable stitches, which need no removal visit; confirm what your child has.
  • Hair usually covers the scar within months (Seattle Children's), and several centres shape zig-zag incisions so the scar hides as hair grows.
  • Ask about sun protection: a fresh scar darkens with sun exposure, which matters in India, so a soft hat outdoors for the early months is a common instruction.
  • The scar looks its worst in week one and improves for many months; photographs taken monthly reassure most parents more than daily inspection.

Ask your team

  • When is the first follow-up?
  • How should the wound or scar be cared for?
  • What activity restrictions apply?
  • When can normal routines resume: bathing, travel, day care?
  • What changes should make us contact the hospital?
  • Are eye, hearing, developmental, dental or other assessments needed?
  • How will skull and head growth be monitored?
  • Who should we contact if we have a concern between appointments?

Some children have regular long-term reviews because certain forms of craniosynostosis can be associated with ongoing needs. Others may have fewer long-term problems.

Do not compare your child's recovery timeline with another child's. A family story can provide emotional reassurance, but it cannot replace the instructions of your child's own surgical team.

First Signs will provide questions, explanations and navigation. It will not provide personalised post-operative instructions.
