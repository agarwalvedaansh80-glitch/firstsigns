Worry and recognising signs

Babies' heads vary enormously and most unusual shapes are not craniosynostosis. What earns a proper look: a firm ridge along a suture line, a shape that is becoming more pronounced month on month, a soft spot that seems absent or closed very early, or a very long-narrow, triangular or one-side-flattened pattern. None of these is a diagnosis; each is a fair reason to ask your paediatrician whether specialist assessment makes sense. Document what you see with dated photographs and bring them. Our Signs page has the full checklist.

Positional flattening comes from how a baby lies and involves no fused suture; it is common, usually improves with repositioning and time, and never needs an operation. Craniosynostosis is the early fusion of a skull suture and is treated by a surgical team. They can look similar in photographs, which is exactly why the distinction is made by clinical examination, not by comparing pictures online. See Craniosynostosis or something else?

No. Every major centre says the same thing, and the Dallas craniofacial centre says it in so many words: mothers should not feel guilty they did anything wrong. Most cases have no identified cause; pressure on the head in the womb and, in a minority, gene changes are the studied factors, and none of them is something a parent chose. The full answer, with sources, is on Causes and genetics.

A fused suture does not reopen with time, massage, oils or repositioning; those help only positional flattening. Some mild cases are monitored rather than operated on, but that is a specialist's active decision with scheduled reviews, not the condition fixing itself. If someone has advised waiting, it is fair to ask what exactly is being watched and when it will be reviewed.

Diagnosis

Examination first. Experienced teams diagnose most cases by examining the head shape, the sutures and the fontanelle and following head growth; the Dallas centre puts examination accuracy at about 98 per cent in experienced hands. Imaging, usually a CT scan, is used to confirm the diagnosis or plan surgery, and centres increasingly use low-dose protocols. Our Diagnosis page lists the questions to ask before any scan.

Centres describe modern craniofacial CT as low-dose, and several (Nationwide, Texas Children's) highlight ultra-low-dose or radiation-free alternatives for follow-up. The useful questions are: what will this scan tell us that examination has not, is a low-dose protocol being used, and will the images be available to other specialists so it does not need repeating? Keep both the report and the images.

It happens in a minority, and it is one of the main reasons treatment exists. Johns Hopkins lists the signs teams watch for: a full or bulging soft spot, unusual sleepiness or irritability, very noticeable scalp veins, a high-pitched cry, poor feeding, projectile vomiting and delayed milestones. If your child shows these signs, contact your team promptly rather than waiting for a routine review.

Surgery decisions

Not every child does; some mild cases are monitored. Where a suture is clearly fused, most centres treat surgically because skull growth is being restricted while the brain is growing fastest. Untreated significant craniosynostosis carries published risks: Johns Hopkins lists headaches, vision, hearing and speech issues, developmental delay and, rarely, pressure damage. The decision is your team's assessment of your child, and 'what happens if we do nothing for six months' is a completely fair question to ask them.

Centres publish different windows, and this is the question where attributed honesty matters most. For endoscopic surgery: Seattle says 4 months or younger, Johns Hopkins up to about 4 months with exceptions to 6, Mayo and Nationwide up to 6 months. For open surgery: Nationwide and Mayo say around or after 6 months, Johns Hopkins 6 to 12 months, GOSH does fronto-orbital work at 12 to 18 months, and the Dallas centre operates at 11 to 15 months, publishing its finding that later operations grew better long term. What every centre agrees on: get assessed early, because early assessment keeps every option open. Full details with sources are on Treatment options.

No. The endoscopic window closes early, but open cranial vault procedures are performed across a wide age range, and one leading centre deliberately operates at 11 to 15 months. Indian series show late presentation is common (a 2025 tertiary-centre study found the average child presenting at almost 11 months), and Indian centres treat older children regularly. The right step is the same at any age: an experienced team's assessment, soon.

They are different tools. Endoscopic surgery uses small incisions, a shorter operation and stay, rarely needs transfusion, and requires months of helmet therapy afterwards; it is generally limited to young infants. Open remodelling reshapes the skull directly in one operation, needs no helmet afterwards at most centres, and is available across a wider age range, with a bigger (usually hair-hidden) scar. One centre on our own panel does not use the endoscopic-plus-helmet route at all, which we disclose openly. The choice depends on your child's age, suture and anatomy, and belongs to your team.

Safety, blood and anaesthesia

The honest published framing: GOSH states on its procedure sheets that the overall risk of a major neurological event or death is much less than one per cent. Named risks that teams discuss include bleeding, infection, cerebrospinal fluid leak and the need for further surgery. One US centre publishes an overall success rate of 95 per cent. Ask your own team for their numbers; a confident team does not mind the question.

It depends on the operation and the centre's technique, and published answers range widely: Mayo says usually needed for open surgery, GOSH says sometimes required with matched blood always kept ready, while the Dallas centre reports under 3 per cent transfusion using pre-operative erythropoietin and cell salvage. For endoscopic surgery, every centre reports transfusion is uncommon. Ask: how often do your patients need blood for this operation, how is blood arranged here, and should we arrange donors in advance? Many Indian hospitals ask families to arrange standby donors as routine.

Paediatric anaesthesia for these operations is given by specialist anaesthetists in children's centres, and craniofacial teams perform them routinely on infants. The anaesthetist will assess your child before the operation and explain fasting rules, monitoring and pain relief. Bring your questions to that conversation: who will be watching my child, what is your experience with this age group, and how is pain managed afterwards? Gillette's published answer on pain is reassuring: narcotics are typically needed only for the first day or so.

Recovery and helmets

Prepare for the first 72 hours so they do not frighten you: a turban-style dressing, possibly small drains behind the ears, and face and eye swelling that typically peaks around day two or three, often swelling the eyes shut, then improves day by day. Gillette and GOSH both describe exactly this arc. Most children are more themselves within days than parents expect. Our After surgery page has the full week-by-week picture and the red flags that should prompt a call.

Published stays: endoscopic, one or two nights (Mayo, CAPPSkids, Seattle); open procedures, about 3 to 5 days at US centres and around 4 days at GOSH, usually with the first night in intensive or high-dependency care; the Dallas centre discharges most children after two nights. Indian stays vary by hospital; ask for the expected stay and the per-day charges in your written estimate.

Only after endoscopic surgery (or separately, for positional flattening). Published wearing time is 22 to 23 hours a day, for anywhere from 3 months to about the first birthday depending on the centre. It must be prescribed and fitted by an orthotist working with your team, never bought online. The full page, including skin care in Indian heat and the cost questions to ask, is here.

Follow your own team's wound instructions first. Published guidance: GOSH allows gentle hair washing with mild shampoo from about day three after spring surgery; Seattle notes the open-surgery scar is usually completely covered by hair within months; several centres use zig-zag incisions so the scar hides in growing hair, and absorbable stitches that need no removal. Sun protection for a fresh scar is worth asking about in India.

Money and logistics in India

No Indian hospital we found publishes its own price. The only concrete public range we could verify (HexaHealth, an aggregator, undated) is Rs 1,00,000 to Rs 2,75,000, and the real number depends on procedure, hospital, city and stay. Treat any figure, including that one, as a starting point and get a written estimate; our Costs page lists exactly what the estimate should cover.

Insurance: under IRDAI's 2019 rules, internal congenital diseases cannot be excluded, external visible anomalies can be, and no public document we found settles which side craniosynostosis falls on, so ask your insurer in writing and mind the waiting periods. PM-JAY: we could not verify a craniosynostosis-named package in the official documents we searched; coverage may still route through general neurosurgery packages at an empanelled hospital, so ask the Ayushman Mitra desk to search the live list in front of you. The step-by-step scripts are on the Costs and Resources pages.

It concentrates in metro centres, and that is normal worldwide. Our doctor panel lists the doctors who have formally agreed to take part in First Signs, with verified, dated credentials, for navigation rather than ranking. When you contact any centre, ask how many craniosynostosis operations the team does a year and how it coordinates care for travelling families.

Ask the scheduler to combine the scan and the surgeon visit into one day (several centres do this for outstation families when asked), request a remote review of existing reports before booking travel, carry every report and the actual scan images, and get the full visit cost in writing beforehand. The travel checklist lives on Finding the right specialist.

The long term

For the common single-suture forms treated in infancy, the published outlook is good: GOSH states children with sagittal synostosis are usually of normal intelligence, one US centre states there is no definitive evidence of IQ impairment in general, and Cleveland Clinic states most children who receive timely treatment have a good prognosis and normal life expectancy. Syndromic forms each have their own picture, laid out plainly on the syndromic page. Development is monitored at follow-up so any support a child needs starts early.

Fused sutures do not simply re-fuse, but a minority of children need further surgery as they grow, for pressure, shape or skull defects. Honest framing: one centre publishes a field-wide claim that up to 40 per cent of children are judged to need repeat procedures, a figure it uses in arguing for its own technique and timing; mainstream hospital pages do not publish comparable numbers, and syndromic children commonly do need planned staged operations. Ask your team what they watch for and how often reoperation happens in their own hands.

Most children return to completely ordinary school lives, and scars usually hide in hair. What helps: agree a one-line answer your child owns ('my head bones joined early and the doctors fixed it'), brief the class teacher once, and treat any teasing as a school behaviour matter early. Our Everyday care page covers siblings, school and confidence in detail.

Until now, mostly in US-run Facebook groups at 2 am.
