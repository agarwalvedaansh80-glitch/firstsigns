Costs, insurance and financial help

Indian families ask about cost before almost anything else, and almost nobody publishes an honest answer. Here is what we can verify, what we cannot, and exactly what to ask, all dated.

What surgery costs in India

No Indian hospital page we found publishes its own craniosynostosis surgery price. The only concrete public range we found (HexaHealth, an aggregator, undated as of 25 September 2026) is Rs 1,00,000 to Rs 2,75,000, with an average around Rs 2,00,000. Treat this as an indicative starting point only: the real number depends on the procedure type, the hospital, the city, the length of stay and your child's needs.

Ask the hospital for a written estimate covering

  • The named procedure your surgeon plans, in writing (the exact words matter for insurance and schemes)
  • Surgeon, anaesthesia, operating theatre and implant or plate charges, if any
  • Expected ICU or high dependency days and ward days, and the per-day rate for each
  • Blood arrangement costs and whether donors are needed
  • Imaging and pre-operative test costs
  • What is excluded, and what a longer stay would cost
  • Costs after discharge: medicines, dressings, follow-up visits, and helmet costs if helmet therapy is prescribed

Private insurance and the congenital condition question

Under the IRDAI's 2019 standardisation of exclusions, internal congenital diseases cannot be excluded from regular health insurance, while external (visible) congenital anomalies remain excludable. Whether an insurer treats craniosynostosis as internal or external is not settled in any public IRDAI or insurer document we could find (searched 25 September 2026), so the answer for your family lives in your policy and your insurer's written replies.

Key point
The waiting-period trap: many policies apply waiting periods of 2 or more years to congenital or pre-existing conditions, while craniosynostosis surgery is typically needed in the first year of life. A policy bought at birth may not clear its waiting period in time. Ask your insurer in writing, before admission, and keep every reply.

Ask your insurer, in writing

  • Is craniosynostosis surgery covered under this policy for my child, treated as an internal congenital condition?
  • Does any waiting period apply, and from what date?
  • Is pre-authorisation required, and what documents does the hospital need to file?
  • Is the planned hospital in the cashless network, or do we claim by reimbursement?
  • What exactly is excluded: helmet therapy, follow-up visits, pre-operative tests?

Government schemes

Craniosynostosis is not among the nine birth defects named under RBSK, and we could not verify a craniosynostosis-named PM-JAY package in the official documents we searched (25 September 2026). That does not always mean no route exists: general neurosurgery packages may apply at an empanelled hospital. The step-by-step questions for RBSK, PM-JAY, MJPJAY and CGHS are on our resources and government pathways page.

If the money is not there

  • Government hospitals: major public teaching hospitals perform this surgery at far lower cost. Waiting lists exist; ask for a written timeline and keep your place while you explore other routes.
  • Scheme desks: the Ayushman Mitra or scheme desk at an empanelled hospital can search live package lists in front of you. Persistence at that desk changes outcomes.
  • Hospital social workers and trusts: some hospitals have charity beds or trust funds; ask the social work department directly whether any apply to paediatric surgery.
  • Crowdfunding: Indian families do fund this surgery through crowdfunding platforms. Before starting, get the hospital's written estimate; campaigns with documents raise more, faster.
  • First Signs: as the programme grows, we aim to guide families to funding routes. We will never promise money we do not have; what we list here will always be what we could verify.
Every figure on this page is indicative and dated. Costs change, schemes change, policies differ. The number that matters is the written estimate from your hospital and the written reply from your insurer or scheme desk.
